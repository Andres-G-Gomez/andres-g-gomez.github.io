The Technical Reality of LLMs for Investment Planning: Why Instruction-Following Models Beat Fine-Tuning in RAG Systems

Imagine you’re a government official tasked with allocating limited resources across healthcare, education, and infrastructure. Traditional planning tools help, but what if AI could synthesize thousands of policy documents, simulate scenarios, and provide contextual recommendations?

After exploring Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) approaches for policy document analysis, a counterintuitive pattern emerges from recent research and practical implementations: fine-tuning language models alongside RAG often hurts more than it helps. Here’s what the technical community is learning about combining these approaches.

The Fine-Tuning + RAG Challenge: Why It’s Harder Than Expected

The Catastrophic Forgetting Problem

When practitioners fine-tune models like GPT-2 on domain-specific documents using techniques like QLoRA, a common pattern emerges: the model learns domain-specific linguistic and semantic patterns, but at the cost of general-purpose reasoning and instruction-following—key traits needed to effectively leverage retrieved context in RAG. Here, we refer specifically to parameter fine-tuning of generation models (e.g. decoder-only LLMs), not embedding models used for retrieval.

While smaller models are especially prone to trade-offs between generality and domain-specificity, the broader challenge is that fine-tuning—even in larger models—can disrupt instruction-following unless it is managed with alignment-preserving techniques. The issue is not just capacity but the fragility of generalization under narrow-task fine-tuning.

While advanced alignment-aware techniques like DPO or instruction-tuned LoRA adapters can mitigate these trade-offs, they remain technically out of reach for many applied teams. For most use cases, strong instruction-tuned models paired with well-engineered prompts outperform domain-specific fine-tuning in RAG systems.

The result? Fine-tuned models often struggle with the instruction-following that RAG systems require. When prompted with “Based on the following context, answer this question…”, they tend to generate domain-relevant text but ignore the retrieval context entirely.

Why Instruction-Tuned Models Often Outperform Custom Fine-Tuning

Models like GPT-4, Claude, or Mixtral-Instruct consistently outperform domain fine-tuned approaches in RAG settings because:

  1. Instruction-Following Architecture: LLMs exhibit reasoning behaviors once thought to be uniquely human, such as identifying causal links from text or generating plausible policy arguments—though these often lack depth or robustness. OPT-IML was specifically trained for this.

  2. Balanced Training: Rather than over-optimizing for domain vocabulary, it maintained the reasoning patterns needed to integrate retrieved information effectively.

  3. Context Handling: Better performance with longer contexts—crucial when RAG systems provide multiple document chunks as evidence.

Advanced Prompt Engineering: The RAG Performance Multiplier

Instead of fine-tuning, sophisticated prompt engineering yielded better results:

Chain-of-Thought for Policy Analysis

Given these World Bank documents about [country], analyze the investment scenario step-by-step:
1. Identify the key economic indicators mentioned
2. Extract successful intervention patterns from similar contexts  
3. Evaluate potential risks and dependencies
4. Recommend the top 3 investment priorities with evidence

Context: [retrieved documents]

Few-Shot Learning with Policy Examples Providing 2-3 examples of high-quality policy analysis in the prompt dramatically improved output quality compared to fine-tuned models that couldn’t generalize beyond their training patterns.

Structured Output Templates Prompts matter: the types of prompts were shown to affect LLM performance. Traditional causal discovery often requires human domain knowledge to complete causal graphs. LLMs show they can be used as a proxy or assistant for humans alongside existing causal methods.

Scenario Simulation: Where Current LLMs Hit Their Limits

The most ambitious application—using LLMs to simulate investment outcomes—highlighted their limitations in causal inference and counterfactual simulation, which go beyond correlation-based reasoning.

The Causal Reasoning Gap

Current evidence indicates that LLMs are only capable of performing shallow (level-1) causal reasoning, primarily attributed to the causal knowledge embedded in their parameters, but they lack the capacity for genuine human-like (level-2) causal reasoning. This aligns with Pearl’s hierarchy of causality and recent empirical work on LLM limitations.

When systems are asked to predict outcomes like “What if we invested $10M in rural healthcare in Kenya?”, they typically generate plausible-sounding responses by pattern matching similar scenarios from training data, but cannot perform genuine counterfactual reasoning. For instance, when asked how $10M in rural healthcare would affect maternal mortality, models may draw from similar-sounding contexts but ignore critical differences in time horizon, local infrastructure, existing program capacity, or political stability—factors essential for accurate policy prediction.

Promising Approaches for Better Causal Reasoning

Recent research suggests several techniques to improve LLM causal capabilities:

  1. Structural Constraint Integration: In one promising technique, LLMs generate initial hypotheses about causal structure, which guide the construction of Dynamic Structural Causal Models (DSCMs). These are refined iteratively, with LLMs serving both as hypothesis generators and critics of the learned model.

  2. LLM-Assisted Causal Discovery: LLMs achieved a remarkable 97% accuracy at determining the causal relationship between variable pairs across over 100 examples from diverse domains like physics, biology, epidemiology and more

  3. Multi-Agent Debate Systems: The debating LLMs papers you referenced show promise. We find that debate consistently helps both non-expert models and humans answer questions, achieving 76% and 88% accuracy respectively By having multiple models argue different positions, we can improve reasoning quality.

Implementing Multi-Agent Policy Simulation

A more sophisticated approach might involve:

  • Agent A: Argues for immediate infrastructure investment
  • Agent B: Advocates for long-term education funding
  • Judge Model: Evaluates arguments using retrieved World Bank outcome data
  • Uncertainty Quantification: DebUnc, a debate framework that uses uncertainty metrics to assess agent confidence. Confidence is then conveyed through a modified attention mechanism that adjusts token weights

Hybrid Approaches: Combining Strengths

Rather than choosing between fine-tuning and RAG, the most promising direction combines multiple techniques:

1. Domain-Adaptive Retrieval

  • Fine-tune embedding models (not generation models) on domain-specific document relationships
  • Use RAG with instruction-following models for generation

2. Structured Knowledge Integration

Leveraging causal knowledge graphs alongside LLMs shows promise for more robust reasoning, as structured frameworks can reduce the interpretability challenges posed by black-box models.

3. Progressive Enhancement

  • Start with strong instruction-following base models
  • Layer on domain-specific prompting strategies
  • Add retrieval augmentation for factual grounding
  • Include uncertainty quantification for high-stakes decisions

Technical Implementation Recommendations

For Investment Analysis RAG Systems:

  1. Use Instruction-Tuned Base Models: Start with models like Claude, GPT-4, or Llama-2-Chat rather than fine-tuning smaller models

  2. Sophisticated Chunking: Beyond simple text splitting, use document structure (sections, tables, policy recommendations) to create semantically coherent chunks

  3. Multi-Modal Retrieval: Combine text with structured data (economic indicators, outcome metrics) for richer context

  4. Confidence Scoring: Implement uncertainty quantification to flag when the model is operating outside its knowledge domain

Prompt Engineering Patterns That Work:

  • Role-based prompting: “You are a World Bank policy analyst…”
  • Evidence requirements: “Cite specific document sections…”
  • Structured reasoning: “First identify the context, then analyze…”
  • Uncertainty acknowledgment: “If uncertain, explain limitations…”

The Path Forward: Practical Recommendations for Policy AI

While LLMs offer significant potential for democratizing policy analysis and reducing dependence on domain experts, the future lies in creating sophisticated AI systems that augment rather than replace human judgment.

RAG System Best Practices Checklist:

  • ✅ Start with instruction-tuned models (GPT-4, Claude, Mixtral-Instruct) rather than domain fine-tuning
  • ✅ Invest in sophisticated chunking based on document structure (sections, policy recommendations, outcome data)
  • ✅ Implement uncertainty quantification to flag when models operate outside their knowledge domain
  • ✅ Use structured prompting patterns (role-based, evidence requirements, step-by-step reasoning)
  • ✅ Combine retrieval with real-time data (economic indicators, outcome metrics) for richer context
  • ✅ Build in human oversight for high-stakes decisions and policy recommendations

Social Impact Potential

These approaches hold particular promise for resource-constrained organizations. NGOs and lower-income governments often lack the budget for extensive policy analysis teams but desperately need evidence-based decision support. A well-designed RAG system could help a ministry of health quickly synthesize lessons from similar interventions across dozens of countries, or enable a local NGO to identify the most effective approaches for their specific context and constraints.

Call for Open Research

The field would benefit from open-source benchmarks like PolicyRAG or CausalBench that specifically test LLM performance on policy reasoning tasks. We need standardized evaluation frameworks that measure not just factual accuracy but also reasoning quality, uncertainty calibration, and real-world applicability.

Bottom Line: For government investment planning, RAG systems with strong instruction-following models and sophisticated prompting outperform fine-tuned approaches while being more accessible to applied teams. The real breakthrough will come from combining retrieval-augmented generation with structured causal reasoning frameworks—turning LLMs from pattern matchers into genuine policy analysis partners that amplify human expertise where it’s needed most.

Technical Implementation Notes

While these insights come from both research literature and practical experimentation with policy document analysis, the fundamental challenges remain consistent across different scales and domains. The key takeaway is that instruction-following capabilities and sophisticated prompting strategies typically outperform naive domain fine-tuning approaches in RAG systems, regardless of whether you’re working with 10 documents or 10,000.
